01 — Foundations

Foundations

What this game is, the core roll, Guard Stat, and a short abstract of every other section.

Five years after the Last War ended, the world hasn't caught its breath. Everbright lanterns still light the streets of Sharn, message stations still hum with House Sivis's magic, and airships still cross the sky on Lyrandar wind — but the peace is thin, the Mournland festers where a nation used to be, and every Dragonmarked House is still quietly maneuvering for the next war nobody's called yet.

You're not a chosen hero. You're a warforged still figuring out what peacetime is for, an elf outcast from two traditions at once, a Cannith heir one bad decision from excoriation, a dust-runner who can price a vein of raw dragonshard at a glance. Whatever you are, you're good at a dangerous job, and Eberron has no shortage of those.

This is a fast, lethal, 2d10 system built on a real gamble at its core: casting means having the shards in hand, right now — reach for more power than you're actually carrying, and that's a genuine risk every time, never a rules footnote. Combat hits hard and heals slow. Guard is your luck and skill holding out under pressure, not meat — and when it runs out, the world remembers.

Core Resolution

PCs are assumed to be competent in their own right, based on their Background and Archetype. Only roll if there's a genuine risk of failure involved. If a character could do something and has the time and resources to do it, let them do it — no roll needed.

The core resolution system is (2d10 + stat) vs Target. Target starts at 12, but can be adjusted per the NPC's own target number, or the scene's target number. This represents the general level of risk a whole moment carries, or an NPC represents, or an aspect of either. That's every check in this system — attacks, saves, skill use, all of it.

What a stat actually represents. Every check happens because something's genuinely at stake, and the stat you roll is the part of your character being tested in that moment — acting on the world, or holding up against something acting on you.

Guard Stat isn't listed here deliberately — every stat is a potential Guard Stat, calculated automatically from whichever one ends up highest and eligible, so it's not a fixed use tied to any one stat the way these are.

Two levers only: Target shift, for baseline difficulty tiers, and Advantage/Disadvantage, for situational swings within an otherwise-normal check.

Shorthand: T+N/T−N. Written elsewhere in this document as a compact way to cite a specific shift. T+N lowers the Target number by N (easier); T−N raises the Target number by N (harder) — the sign tracks whether it's good or bad for whoever's rolling, not the direction the Target number itself moves, so T−2 means Target 14, not Target 10. T+2 matches the +2 row below, T−2 matches the −2 row.

Guidance on picking a shift: reach for ±1 or ±2 when only one or two factors are actually in play. Save ±3 for situations genuinely matching one of the three named tiers, or where multiple stacking factors would have justified a combined total — shift once by the combined total rather than stacking multiple separate shifts.

A roll should only ever add the character's own stat bonus. External difficulty lives in the target number or in Adv/Disadv, never as a second flat modifier bolted onto the dice total.

Advantage/Disadvantage and Target shifts represent different kinds of change, not interchangeable ways to say the same thing. Advantage is temporary — tied to a specific moment or a resource only one character has right then: a moment of surprise, a Talent triggering, terrain someone specifically exploited. It doesn't require rethinking the encounter, just recognizing this one roll is different. A Target shift is a baseline — hard to move without changing the whole scene or the nature of the NPC involved, though it can still move. It's not won mid-fight by a clever action; it's set in advance, as preparation, leveling the playing field in a lasting way for the whole encounter or moment, not just one roll.

Advantage / Disadvantage: roll 3d10 instead of 2d10, keep the best two (Advantage) or worst two (Disadvantage). Disadvantage rolls never crit.

Critical Hit. If both counted dice on a successful roll show the same value, it's a critical — maximum damage, no damage roll at all. Applies identically to weapon attacks and Attack spells, since both roll to-hit the same way. Advantage can still crit if those two kept dice match. Disadvantage never crits, under any circumstance — even if the two worst dice happen to match each other, it's just a hit, not a critical.

Damage Floor. Any successful hit deals a minimum of 1 damage, regardless of die result or negative stat bonus.

Situational difficulty works two ways only: shift the Target number, or grant Advantage/Disadvantage. A GM never stacks small numeric modifiers on top of a roll — every other number on a character sheet stays fixed; only the target and the dice pool ever move.

Advantage and Disadvantage never stack within a single roll, from any combination of sources. A given roll has Advantage, Disadvantage, or neither. If one specific roll would qualify for both at once, they cancel — roll normally. This is strictly per-roll, not per-character — a PC's own outgoing attack and an enemy's incoming attack against that same PC are separate rolls that never interact, even in the same round.

Worked Example — Core Resolution

Rivet (STR +2) swings a Heavy weapon at a Standard-tier bandit (Target 12). He rolls 2d10: 6 and 9, totaling 15. Add his +2 bonus: 17, comfortably clearing 12 — a hit. Later, Rivet is Reeling (Disadvantage) from an open Wound and attacks again: he rolls three dice instead of two — 4, 8, 3 — and keeps the worst two (4 and 3), totaling 7. Add +2: 9. Short of 12 — a miss.

Guard Stat

Every character has a damage buffer called Guard (full explanation on Combat). It's not chosen — it's calculated automatically: your highest-bonus stat, excluding your cast stat (and Wandslinger stat, if taken) and the governing stat of every weapon currently equipped in a hand slot. CON is never a cast or weapon stat, so it's always eligible.

Guard Stat (if it lands there)Concept
STR — Brute ForcePhysically blocks and overpowers incoming attacks (only possible if STR isn't your weapon or cast stat)
DEX — ReflexesDodges and weaves; full hits become glancing ones (only possible if DEX isn't your weapon or cast stat)
CON — EnduranceClassic tank: physically tough, shrugs off blows
INT — TacticsReads the fight, positions so blows land where they hurt least
WIS — AwarenessDanger sense; flinches away before the worst lands
CHA — PresenceForce of personality throws off an attacker's aim and timing

Every weapon is governed by STR or DEX, so at least one of them is always excluded by whatever's equipped — the other stays genuinely eligible unless it's also your cast stat, or you're dual-wielding across both (a melee weapon and a ranged one at once excludes both).

Stable by default, not frozen — it only changes as a direct consequence of changing what's equipped. Nothing recalculates on its own turn to turn; there's no passive drift and no free re-optimizing. But producing or stowing an item is always a full action, and if that specific change alters which stats are excluded, Guard Stat recalculates immediately — mid-combat included: freeing a stat moves it up, conflicting with a new weapon moves it down, and if it moves down, current Guard drops to match the new max immediately, floor 0. One exception: the Heavy/Medium armor CON requirement stays tied to actual CON regardless of Guard Stat — poor CON locks out Heavy armor even if CON isn't your Guard Stat.

Magic

Possession-based casting (you need enough shards on hand, not a daily spell-slot budget), six potency tiers from free Cantrips to world-altering Mythic effects, and the Overcast Backlash Pool for attempting a cast you can't quite afford. Example: a Minor-tier attack spell deals 1d6 + your cast stat, identical in shape to a Medium weapon — magic and steel compete on the same terms. Full page →

Combat

Guard-and-Wound HP model, zone-based positioning, weapon tiers, and the full Wayfarer Grit Maneuvers list. Example: a Wound reduces your max Guard by 2, and current Guard refills to that new, lower max — you're worse off going forward, not just hurt right now. Full page →

Exploration
